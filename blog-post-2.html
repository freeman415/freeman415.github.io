Blog Post #2: Fixed Gear Clips

Here is a preview of some of the footage: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-giyBY_zyHc

For this blog post, my video idea was to collect fixed gear bike clips. A fixed gear bike, often called a fixie, is a bike where the pedals are directly connected to the back wheel. This means the rider cannot coast like they would on a normal bike. If the wheel is moving, the pedals are moving too. Because of this, fixed gear riding has its own style and takes a lot of control. One of the main tricks people do on fixed gear bikes is skidding. Skidding is when the rider locks up the back wheel and slides across the ground, either to slow down, stop, or add style to the ride.

For the filming process, I recorded my friend Ruben riding his bike and doing tricks. I wanted the clips to show the speed and movement of fixed gear riding instead of just filming him from one spot. To do that, I rode my own bike while recording him so I could get follow-along shots. This made the video feel more active because the camera was moving with him. I think this helped capture the feeling of actually riding next to someone instead of just watching from far away.

The hardest part was trying to record while also riding my bike. I had to pay attention to Ruben, the camera, my own balance, and the space around me at the same time. Since we were moving fast, it was difficult to keep the video steady and keep Ruben in the frame. It also would not have worked as well if the camera was standing still, because a still camera would not show the full effect of how fast we were moving. The movement of the camera helped make the clips feel more realistic and exciting.

Another challenge was that the ground was still wet from previous rain. Since fixed gear bikes already slide when skidding, the wet pavement made everything more slippery than usual. This made the tricks look interesting, but it also made filming more difficult and a little more risky. From this activity, I learned that filming action videos takes more planning than I expected. I learned that camera movement can change the feeling of a video, and that sometimes an imperfect, shaky shot can actually make the video feel more real.
